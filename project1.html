UX Research · Case Study 01

Fixing a food delivery app's invisible failure.

Users were successfully placing orders to addresses outside the delivery zone — and only finding out when the rider called to cancel. This is the story of how I found the root cause and redesigned the experience to prevent it.

01 — Problem statement

The app promises delivery it can't actually fulfil.

This project started with my own frustration. I placed an order through a popular food delivery app, received an order confirmation, and waited — only for the delivery rider to call and tell me my address was outside their serviceable range.

The app had accepted my address, processed my payment, and confirmed my order without ever checking whether delivery was actually possible. The failure happened at the worst moment: after commitment, after payment, after waiting.

This isn't just a bad experience. It erodes trust. If an app can't tell me it won't deliver to my house until after the order is placed, why would I trust anything else it tells me?

⚠️

Late-stage failure

The error surfaces only after an order is placed and payment is taken — the worst possible moment.

🔇

No upfront warning

There is no pre-order check, alert, or indication that the address might be outside the service zone.

📉

Trust collapse

Users feel deceived. The app appeared to accept their address, so the cancellation feels like a broken promise.

🔁

No graceful recovery

After cancellation, there is no guided path to find a nearby restaurant that can deliver, or to update the address.

02 — Research goals

What I set out to understand and achieve.

Before touching any design, I defined two layers of goals: what I needed to learn through research, and what I wanted the redesigned experience to achieve.

Research goal 1: Understand whether this was an isolated incident or a pattern experienced by other users in different locations.

Research goal 2: Identify at what stage in the ordering journey users expect location availability to be confirmed.

Research goal 3: Understand how users respond emotionally and behaviourally after a failed delivery — do they retry, switch apps, or abandon?

Design goal 1: Prevent failed orders by validating location availability before the user reaches checkout.

Design goal 2: Communicate service limitations clearly and early, without disrupting the experience for users in-range.

Design goal 3: Provide a helpful recovery path if a location isn't serviceable, rather than a dead end.

03 — Research process

Listening before designing.

I spoke with 6 frequent food delivery app users across different parts of Chennai. Rather than jumping to solutions, I wanted to first understand their mental models: what do users expect the app to verify, and when?

I used three methods in combination, deliberately chosen to triangulate findings. No single method tells the whole story.

01

User interviews

30-minute semi-structured conversations exploring past ordering experiences, frustrations, and expectations. I asked about both positive and negative experiences to avoid leading the narrative.

02

Observation

I watched 3 participants place a real order while thinking aloud. This revealed gaps between what users say they do and what they actually do — including that most skip past the address confirmation screen.

03

Quick survey

A brief 5-question survey to quantify how common the problem was. 4 out of 6 interview participants reported experiencing at least one failed or cancelled order due to location issues.

"I assumed if I could type my address in, it would work. No one checks a map when ordering food."

— Interview participant, age 28

04 — Key insights

What the research revealed.

These four findings directly shaped every design decision that followed. Each one pointed to a mismatch between user expectations and system behaviour.

Users assume address acceptance equals delivery confirmation. If the app accepts their address, they believe it can deliver. No one reads this as "address saved" — they read it as "order will arrive."

Location is checked too late in the current flow. By the time the system flags an issue, users have already invested time choosing items, reviewing their cart, and entering payment. The failure feels like a breach of contract.

The error message is vague and unhelpful. "Delivery not available" tells users nothing. They don't know if it's a temporary issue, a zone boundary, or something they can fix by changing their address.

Failed orders significantly reduce app trust and re-use. 3 out of 6 participants said they switched to a competitor after a failed delivery — not because the alternative was better, but because it felt safer.

05 — Design decisions

The "why" behind every choice.

Good UX design isn't about what you build — it's about why. Here are the key decisions I made and the reasoning that drove each one.

Decision 01

Validate location immediately after address entry — not at checkout.

The research showed users' mental model is clear: if I can type it, it should work. Moving validation to the address stage (before browsing begins) aligns the system's behaviour with that expectation. It also prevents wasted time — users won't browse menus they can't order from.

Decision 02

Use a persistent, non-blocking banner — not an interruptive modal.

A modal would feel alarming and stop the user cold. But a silent warning gets ignored. A persistent banner in the address bar — visible but not disruptive — surfaces the information exactly when users will look for it (when confirming their address) without hijacking the experience.

Decision 03

Replace vague error copy with a specific, actionable message.

"Delivery not available" closes a door. "Your address is 2.3km outside our current delivery zone. Try a nearby pickup location?" explains the problem and gives the user somewhere to go. Specificity rebuilds trust where vagueness destroys it.

Decision 04

Offer a recovery path, not just a rejection.

Users who hit a dead end leave. Users who are shown an alternative (pickup option, different address, estimated expansion date) have a reason to stay. The goal is to keep the user in the app, not just technically fix the validation error.

06 — Final solution

Before vs. after: the redesigned flow.

The redesign shifts location validation from the end of the journey to the beginning — and replaces a dead-end error with a helpful, actionable response.

07 — Testing & outcome

What happened when users tested the new flow.

I presented the redesigned flow to 3 users using a paper prototype in Figma. Each participant walked through both the old and new flows and was asked to compare their experience.

3/3

Users found the new flow clearer and more trustworthy

100%

Said they would continue using the app after seeing a clear zone warning

0

Users confused about whether their address was serviceable in the new flow

Qualitatively, users described the new flow as "honest," "like it cares about my time," and "way less stressful." One participant specifically noted that knowing upfront felt respectful — it didn't waste their time building an order that would fail.

Limitation: This was a small, informal test with 3 participants using a prototype — not a live product. Quantitative impact metrics would require A/B testing in a production environment, which is a clear next step.

08 — Reflection & learnings

What I'd do differently, and what I'll carry forward.

This project taught me as much about how to do UX research as it taught me about the specific problem I was solving.

✓

Observation revealed what interviews didn't.

In interviews, several participants said they "always check the address before placing an order." Observation showed the opposite — they skip right past it. This gap between stated and actual behaviour was the most valuable insight of the project.

✓

The "why" behind design decisions matters as much as the decisions themselves.

Early in the project, I was tempted to jump to "add a warning message." But without understanding the mental model mismatch, I would have placed that warning in the wrong part of the journey. Research changed both what I designed and where.

✓

Scale doesn't diminish validity — but it limits confidence.

Six interviews and three tests is not a large sample, and I'm careful not to over-generalise. But the consistency of findings across participants gave me enough confidence to move forward. Next time, I'd supplement with a larger survey to quantify the scope of the problem.

✓

UX improvements that benefit users often benefit business metrics too.

Reducing failed orders is better for users — but it also reduces cancellations, customer service contacts, refund costs, and churn. The best UX solutions aren't trade-offs between user and business goals; they serve both.

What comes next

If this moved to production, I'd push for live A/B testing to measure the real impact on order success rates, user retention, and support tickets — turning qualitative insight into quantitative proof.
